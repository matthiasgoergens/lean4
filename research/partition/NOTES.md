# Partition schemes for the Lean pdq: notes

Research for a possible follow-up to the qsort work (branch `matthias/qsort`): a partition as fast as the branch-free one-directional loop on random keys that keeps pdq's two-ended partition's speed on run-structured input.  Run on the benchmark machine, CPUs 40–45, from a worktree of the research harness (branch `matthias/qsort-partition`).

Tools here: `model.py` is a step-for-step Python model of `PdqLab.sort` (all knobs except `block`), instrumented by phase (comparisons in partitions, pivot choice, partial insertion sort, insertion sort, heapsort, equal partitions; swaps; partition sizes; heapsort fallbacks).  It is checked against the Lean binary by the harness's `pair` hash, which records how equal keys were permuted: identical on every informative case (dups4, organ, dupsqrt at 20,000 elements, for pdq, `lab-c24`, `lab:lomuto,c24`, `lab:lomuto,bl,c24`, `lab:hyb,bl,c24`, `lab:pairs,c24`, `lab:m3,nochild`, and later `lab:bh`, `lab:bh,c24`, `lab:brk9`, `lab:lomuto,bl,c24,brk9`).  `trace.py` and `trace2.py` print the partition sequence.

## Question 1: why one-directional partitions lose on interleaved runs

**Not the micro-architecture: more work.**  `perf stat` on the box (1,000,000 `nat`, runs16, subtracting `none`): pdq executes 0.94 G instructions in the sort, `lab:lomuto,bl,c24` 1.72 G (1.8×) and `lab:lomuto,c24` 1.59 G; on random keys the branch-free loop executes 1.13× pdq's instructions in half its cycles.  A profile by function (`perf record -e instructions`) puts 21% of the branch-free variant's instructions in heapsort (`siftDownM` under `heapSortM`); pdq has none.  (`perfstat1-runs.txt`: `perf stat -r 10` on CPU 40, binary built from commit 5366ee2's sources; `none` subtracted by hand.)

**Mechanism: a self-similar fixed point that defeats the pivot sample.**  The one-directional partitions (branchy or not) keep the elements less than the pivot in their original order.  On `runsK` (K ascending runs of equal length, each spanning the whole key range) with K a multiple of 4, the ninther's three groups sit a few positions before the ends of runs K/4−1, K/2−1 and 3K/4−1 (the pattern's run length is ⌊n/K⌋+1, so a quarter of the range falls (K/4)(⌊n/K⌋+1) − n/4 positions before a run boundary: 4 for runs16 at 100,000).  Those samples are among the largest of their runs, so the pivot is near the maximum and the partition removes the top few elements of every run.  Because the less side keeps its order, it is again K equal runs, shorter by the same amount each, and the distance from each quarter point to the run end is unchanged (each run lost c elements, the range lost Kc, the quarter point moved by Kc/4, exactly as far as the run boundary).  So every following partition is equally bad: `trace2.py lab:lomuto,bl,c24 runs16 100000 30000` shows 16 consecutive partitions each keeping 99.7% of the range, until the limit runs out and heapsort takes 33,803 elements.  pdq's breakPatterns (Go's) only randomises the middle group, so the two outer groups still outvote it.  pdq's two-ended partition does not keep order: it swaps misplaced elements from the far end into the run ends, so the next samples land elsewhere and the imbalance decays in one or two steps (0.9989, 0.9855, 0.824, ...).

**Evidence, model, 100,000 elements** (`stats-phases-100k.txt`): heapsort takes 23% (runs4), 34% (runs16) and 57% (runs64) of the elements under the branch-free one-directional loop, none under pdq's partition; that is 4–16 comparisons per element of the 9–16 extra.  **Prediction tested** (`predict-k.txt`): run counts that are not multiples of 4 should not trigger the fixed point.  runs6, runs7, runs10: no heapsort, comparisons equal to pdq's (18.3 vs 18.4 per element etc.).  runs8, runs12, runs20, runs32: heapsort again (runs20: 97% of the elements).  Confirmed.

**A second, smaller effect: failed partial insertion sorts.**  runs5 has no fixed point but the branch-free loop spends 10.8 comparisons per element in partial insertion sorts that fail, against 2.3 under pdq's partition: the order-keeping less side looks ascending to the next pivot sample, so the smaller child (which pdq always lets try) attempts the partial insertion sort, and it fails after up to five long moves.

**A third: per-element cost on presorted input.**  On nearly sorted, organ pipe and sorted-plus-tail input both partitions do about the same comparisons (model), but pdq's scans are perfectly predicted and swap almost nothing, while the branch-free loop writes two elements per element (swaps 8.1 vs 3.8, 16.7 vs 6.6, 3.0 vs 0.1 per element).  That is round ten's 1.15–1.35× there.

**The laptop.**  The first two effects are counts of work, independent of the machine, so "no loss on the Intel laptop" (round nine's note) should not be: not re-checked here (the work runs on the box), worth re-checking with the same n before believing it.

## Question 2: why the adjacent-pairs choice blows up

The same fixed point, entered earlier and caught bigger.  `lab:pairs,c24` uses pdq's partition only where 8 sampled adjacent pairs are in order.  On runs the first partition (pairs in order: two-ended) has a pivot near the maximum and leaves the run structure; its swaps put a few descending pairs into the range, so from then on the branch-free loop is chosen and the fixed point holds: on runs4 at 100,000 heapsort takes 99.7% of the elements after 17 unbalanced partitions; on runs64 at 1,000,000 it takes 88% (`trace2.py lab:pairs,c24 runs64 1000000 100000`).  On the box (`perf stat`, sort time with `none` subtracted): runs4 at 100,000 2.3× pdq, runs64 at 1,000,000 2.6×; runs16 and runs64 at 100,000 are about level.  The 5–9× reported earlier was not reproduced at these sizes and is not in any saved log.

## Question 3: candidates

- **`brk9`** (new knob): breakPatterns swaps pseudo-random elements into all three ninther groups, not only the middle.  Removes the fixed point: no heapsort on runs4/16/20/64 in the model, comparisons 21–23 per element (pdq's partition: 20–28).  On the box (`perfstat-screen1.txt`, instructions, which load does not distort): runs16 1.70 G vs 2.25 G without it (pdq 1.47 G); runs4 1.88 G vs 2.76 G (pdq 1.64 G); runs64 1.70 G vs 2.02 G (pdq 1.45 G).  Does not touch the partial-insertion-sort effect (runs5).
- **`hyb,bl,brk9`**: pdq's scan where the pivot sample looked ascending, the branch-free loop elsewhere, plus `brk9`.  Model: equal to pdq on nearly sorted and sorted-plus-tail (it uses pdq's scan there), no heapsort on runs, runs5 back to pdq's comparisons (the failed partial insertion sort happens once, then pdq's scan scrambles the order), but more comparisons on the ninther killer (12.9 vs 8.9 per element).
- **`bh`** (new knob, `hoareBranchless`): pdq's two-ended partition one step at a time, branch-free: compare `v[i]` and `v[j]`, swap `v[i]` with `v[i + s(j−i)]` (s = 1 when both misplaced), advance `i` and `j` by 0/1 selects.  It swaps exactly pdq's pairs (model and Lean `pair` hashes equal pdq's), so it inherits pdq's recursion: no fixed point, no partial-insertion-sort effect, and its correctness argument is Hoare's invariant (`[a+1, i)` less, `(j, b)` not less).  Getting Lean to compile it without branches took two rewrites: `&&`, `||` and `Bool.toNat` compile to nested branches and a call; choosing between the index objects `i` and `j` compiles to a branch per arm because each takes its own reference; constants chosen by `if c then 1 else 0` and `i + s * (j - i)` compile to selects.  Then it mispredicts no more than the one-directional loop (1.5 M on 1,000,000 random `nat`), **but it loses**: sort cycles 0.76× pdq on random `nat` (the one-directional loop: 0.44×), 1.6× pdq on runs16, 1.25× on random records.  It compares about 1.33 times per element on random keys and up to 2 on runs (the element on the side that did not move is compared again), and Lean's per-access overheads make each step long.  A negative result for this formulation.

**Why the remaining cells lose, and two fixes** (model `stats-cap.txt`, `stats-pisx.txt`; counters `perfstat-screen6.txt`, `perfstat-screen7.txt`):

- *runs with small odd counts, the ninther killer*: the failed partial insertion sorts of the first stage.  A per-path budget of failed attempts (`capN`, model only) just trades runs5 against nearly sorted again (budget 1: runs5 fixed, nearly 14.4 comparisons per element against 7.9; budget 3: nearly fine, runs5 halfway).  **`pisx`**: Go's partial insertion sort also gives up, before moving anything, at a descent `v[i] < v[i-1]` whose neighbours are inverted too (`v[i+1] < v[i-2]`).  That is a boundary between two ordered blocks, which five moves cannot repair; an isolated misplaced element (nearly sorted) leaves `v[i-2] ≤ v[i+1]`.  Model, comparisons per element (pdq / `ends,brk9` / `ends,brk9,pisx`): runs5 20.6 / 28.9 / 20.6, runs9 21.9 / 24.7 / 19.7, nearly 9.1 / 7.9 / 6.2, ninther 8.9 / 9.8 / 8.4, everything else unchanged. - *runs3 (1.13 even with `pisx`) and `u64` ninther at 100,000 (1.15)*: not the partial insertion sort (runs3: 10 attempts in the model), but the first stage's third effect, the per-element cost of the branch-free loop where pdq's branches are predicted (pdq mispredicts 0.26 M branches on runs3 against 0.78 M).  **`pred`**: after the end scans, count how often "less than the pivot" flips across the 16 elements at each end of the middle; at most 3 flips (random data flips about every other element) sends the middle to pdq's own loop, continuing from where the scans stopped.  Box sort cycles vs pdq for `ends,brk9,pisx,pred`: runs3 1.05, `u64` ninther 100k 0.98, nearly 1M 0.99, organ 0.99, ninther 1M 0.96, runs5 0.94, runs16 0.86, runs64 0.77, sortedtail 0.89, dups4 0.40, random nat 0.46, records 0.86.

Correctness for both: model and Lean `pair` hashes agree; `sorttest` 460,000 cases, 0 failures; harness check on 23 patterns × 5 types × 8 sizes for `pisx` variants, 0 failures (the `pred` variant is in `sorttest` and the round below runs unchecked with hashes compared).  Argument: `pisx` only makes an optional partial insertion sort give up earlier; `pred` hands pdq's own loop the state its scans would have reached (`partitionM.loop` from `i+1, j-1` after swapping `v[i]`, `v[j]`), so both keep the existing contracts.

### Conclusions of the third stage

1. **Level-varying sample positions do not earn their keep.**  On their own they remove the run fixed point (no heapsort fallback on runs4/16/64 without `brk9`), which is what they were meant to do.  But spread-out samples are worse medians than the quarter points on several ordinary shapes, even for pdq's own two-ended partition: runs3 (the quarter points take one sample per run, at 1/4, 1/2 and 3/4 of their ranges; spread samples land in two runs), the ninther killer and m3killer, at 3–6% more instructions on random keys.  `brk9` with the quarter points removes the fixed point with neither cost.  Mixing the start into the seed (`seeda`) softens the ninther killer but costs sorted-plus-tail; it does not earn its keep against the length-only seed either.  They cannot replace `pisx6`, which addresses something else (the partial insertion sort), and `pisx6` should go anyway: it turns sorted-plus-a-small-block inputs into n log n (the review's tail/midblock/appended finding, confirmed in the model).
2. **The order-keeping partition is not safe as a general-purpose replacement.**  After the principled changes (fixed quarter points with `brk9`; flip windows placed inside the middle, `predm`, instead of the `pisx6` special case), it still loses by more than 1.15× on must-pass shapes: m3killer (1.8× in instructions and comparisons, 1.5–1.9× in three timings) and inputs hill-climbed against an earlier variant (1.26–1.31× comparisons at 2,000, 1.14× at 20,000).  m3killer is not a pivot problem (every partition is balanced for both algorithms): pdq's two-ended pairing happens to sort whole subranges of Musser's perfect shuffle, and an order-keeping partition cannot reproduce that.  Hill climbing against B would very likely find more such inputs.  By the author's stopping rule, no further special cases; this is the clean negative.
